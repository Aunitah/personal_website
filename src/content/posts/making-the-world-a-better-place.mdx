---
title: "Making The World A Better Place"
date: "2025-01-30"
---

Any soul who knew her was blessed by her generosity, kindness, optimism, and warmth.

Death has always felt intangible to me, especially when the one who leaves this world is someone woven into your heart. You do not know how to face the sudden emptiness they leave behind. It feels like being shot in the stomach, a sharp pain followed by the hollow, the ache, the strange silence where their presence used to rest. It feels as if they take a piece of you with them. For me, that person was my grandmother, my Maman jan.

These past five years, I have lost so much by not feeling her touch, not sitting beside her, not being held in her presence. When I left Iran, all I could think about was my own departure. I never allowed myself to imagine that the people I loved would someday depart from this world. Mamajan was my fiercest supporter before I left, after I left, and through every difficult moment. Her dream was simple and enormous. She wanted her children and grandchildren to grow, to learn, and to reach the brightest futures she was never given a chance to imagine for herself.

She died, this Thursday, in ICU.

Just this week, right before this, during my trip to Germany, I felt a kind of peace I had not felt in years. School was steady, work was steady, my love life felt whole, the fear of war in Iran had softened, and even my own body felt stronger. I walked through Europe and saw beauty more clearly than ever before. For the first time in a long time, I felt myself returning to myself. And then, within that moment of quiet calm, Mamajan left this world. It felt as if she had waited. It felt as if she needed to see me safe and steady and no longer inside her worry. It felt as if her prayers for me, at least for now, had reached their end.

Who was my Maman jan, and how much do we owe her?

Before she was my Maman jan, she was a little girl named Shavande Gol. She was born into the Bakhtiary tribe, the nomads of Iran who lived on the land, moved with their herds, and followed the seasons. At fourteen she married my Babajan.

She finished her education in fifth grade and became a mother at fourteen when she gave birth to my oldest aunt. Knowing her only as my grandmother, a woman shaped by age and wisdom, it aches me to imagine her as a young girl holding a newborn in her arms. Sometimes I wish I could reach back in time and hold that fourteen year old girl myself and tell her she deserved gentleness. She went on to have five daughters and one son.

My grandfather had completed high school, something he carried with quiet pride because he worked hard to provide for all his siblings during the day and attended classes at night. He was later hired by Iran's oil and gas industry and trained by British engineers. Each year he worked harder, rose a little higher, and slowly improved their lives.

They lived in the south of Iran near the oil fields. Babajan was always traveling for work, and Maman jan raised six children almost entirely on her own. The patriarchy of that time made her life even harder, yet she still created a home filled with stability, purpose, and dignity. She had no education beyond elementary school, but she valued learning more than anything. Babajan valued education too, but it was Maman jan who made sure it actually happened. She pushed, protected, encouraged, and sacrificed until every one of her children found their place.

All six children graduated from high school. Many graduated at the top of their class. All six earned at least a bachelor's degree. They studied chemistry, public health, occupational health, computer science, accounting, and medicine, which was my mother's path.

Maman jan poured her life into raising her children. She raised them to be educated, respected, and independent. She raised them to have voices and to never give in. Everything began with her.

How did I know Mamajan?

After the Iran Iraq war in the 1980s, my mother's family moved from the south of Iran to Esfahan, the city where I would later be born. Esfahan was larger and more modern, with better schools and more opportunity, and it became the place where all of Mamajan's children completed their education. Before the move, they had owned a spacious house with a wide garden, but the war and the economic collapse that followed the revolution nearly wiped out their savings. They sold the house and bought a smaller apartment on the second floor of a modest two story building. Mamajan never liked it. Her legs had carried the weight of years of housework, and the climb up those stairs hurt her for as long as I can remember.

My parents bought a home only two streets away from hers. One of my aunts lived directly above us, and another lived almost next door to Mamajan. We grew up inside a circle of houses that always pointed back to her. She raised all of us grandchildren. She potty trained us. She fed us. She soothed us. She held us when our parents were tired or overwhelmed. She took care of me and my brother especially, since my mother worked long hours as a physician and was rarely home during the day.

My brother and I ate at her house almost every day. Her home was the center of our childhood, the place where we were shaped, fed, comforted, and loved. Every warm memory from my early years begins inside her walls.

When our parents argued, or when we became teenagers and argued with them ourselves, Mamajan's home became our refuge. Her door was always open. A warm meal waited for anyone who stepped inside, not only her own grandchildren but anyone who needed a moment of peace. This is what I remember most about her. Every person who knew her carried a piece of her kindness and generosity with them, as if her warmth lived on in the people she touched.

Her Superpower:

One of her most unforgettable traits was her quiet way of giving. She practiced a kind of silent philanthropy. Sometimes she would slip money into my jacket pocket without telling me. When I found it and tried to thank her, she would quickly hush me. She helped families and children without ever speaking about it. When she supported parents who were struggling, she made sure their children never saw the worry or the shame. She brought food without being asked. She helped without drawing attention to herself. From her, I learned the power of kindness that makes no noise. The kind that does not announce itself. The kind that simply fills the room.

I always felt safe around her. People might have called her a housewife, but she was one of the greatest philanthropists I have ever known. She changed so many lives, both near and far. Beside her, my Babajan built free schools and hospitals in villages throughout Iran. Being selfless and humble was part of who they were, and I grew up believing that this was simply how the world worked.

As I grew older, I realized an important truth. The people who help the most are rarely the richest. They are the ones who know how it feels to be helpless. They climb one step above that helplessness, find themselves in a slightly better place, and treat that place as a privilege to be shared. Mamajan knew what it meant to go to sleep hungry. She knew what it meant to grow up with nothing. So she shared everything she had, always making sure that no one around her went without a meal.

Half of her children immigrated out of Iran. The rest stayed, including my mother. Every one of her grandchildren eventually left Iran, including me. Yet we were her greatest joy. She called us constantly. She had a habit of sitting on the right side of her couch and phoning each grandchild one by one, starting with the ones who lived outside Iran. Before I left, I used to lie next to her and watch her do this. Eventually my name joined that list. I was her youngest grandchild.

I still have so many of her voicemails. They are from the times I did not pick up because I was in class or at work. Now I wish I had answered every single one. I wish I had paused my work, stepped out of the lecture hall, and heard her voice. I lost so much these past five years by not seeing her.

She was always well dressed and beautifully put together. She cared about her skin and never left the house without her dark red lipstick. She cooked for others at all times. She tried to stay optimistic even when life was heavy. These traits live in me now. Anything good in me comes from watching her, learning from her, growing beside her for so many years.

Life After Maman jan:

My family says she was the light of our lives, and now that she is gone, the light has gone with her. But on the day she died, something strange and gentle happened. I was in Germany, and for the first time on that trip the sky opened and the sun came out. Since then, very city I visited after that became sunny. It felt as if she had become the sun itself. It felt as if her light had simply moved to the sky.

Nearly one thousand people are mourning her loss this week. She was so kind that if she knew we would be this heartbroken, she would have tried to stay a little longer. She was the light of my world. It feels as though she took a part of me with her, yet from that missing piece I can feel flowers beginning to bloom. She nurtured my soul so I could grow into the kind of human being she was. When she saw that I was finally ready, she left, taking the shelter of her presence with her.

She is the sun now. The shelter is gone, but the light remains. With her light, the flowers and trees inside me can bloom and grow. I can try to become the kind of soul she was for me and for everyone she touched. She always told me to be strong. To keep going. To be a goer, a runner of life. She would tell me, you are Aunita, you can do this, you are the best, be the best.

She was the warmest shelter and the strongest shelter I have ever known. She will not call me again. She will not be here to hear my achievements and be filled with joy. The Mamajan of our big and beautiful family is gone. But her absence also asks something of us. We can each become a little bit of her for someone else. We can keep our doors open to those in need. We can move through life with kindness, forgiveness, community, and humanity. We can choose to make an impact in the lives around us. In our own ways, we can all become a Mamajan. No matter how difficult or unkind the world has been to us, we can always choose to disagree with that darkness and offer light instead.

People like Mamajan are never gone. I feel her alive in my heart every day. Life continues because of people like her. Their kindness moves quietly from one generation to the next, and humanity survives because of the goodness they leave behind.

They do truly make the world a better place

and I will spend my life trying to do the same.

-From the luckiest granddaughter of a little girl from the Bakhtiary tribe.

Aunita
